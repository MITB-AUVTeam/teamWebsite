Every AUV needs a brain, but a brain is useless without a nervous system. After talking it through with Azad and Chatur, that's basically what this board had to be, a central hub tying our microcontroller to the BNO055 IMU, five ESCs for the thrusters, and the MS5837 pressure sensor. Three completely different things to wire up, on one small board, with no room for a loose connection once it's underwater.

# Ugly Before Clean

We didn't start with a PCB. Honestly it felt out of reach at the point we were still figuring out how everything should even connect, and I'd never designed a PCB before, let alone all by myself. Fab houses don't do same day returns and we were on a tight budget, so getting it wrong wasn't really an option. Perfboard meant if a connection was bad I could just rip it out and redo it in the time it takes to reheat a soldering iron. So we just went with it, wires everywhere, masking tape doing the job of proper strain relief, and half the pin mapping just existing in my head because there was nowhere else to write it. It had to be ugly before it could be clean.

# The GPIO That Cried Wolf

Midway through, our original RP2040 based Vicharak board just started failing. GPIO pins ghosting us for no obvious reason. We didn't have time to sit and debug it properly since we were already close to the submission deadline, so we switched over to the Waveshare RP2350-Zero instead. Cheaper, more reliable, and it just worked. It sits at the bottom edge of the perfboard with the USB-C left exposed so we can reflash it easily. We left the old female headers for the Vicharak on the board too, mostly in case the RP2350 acted up as well, and it saved us some time not desoldering them. Five 2-pin JST connectors went to the ESCs, one 4-pin JST for the IMU, and another 4-pin JST for the pressure sensor. I did try to keep the wire colours consistent though, yellow, blue, green for signal, red and black for power and ground, a habit from the TinkerCAD sims the seniors made us do during task phase.

# Notebook Before KiCad

Before I touched any PCB software I sat down and just hand drew the whole thing in my notebook. Part of it was that I didn't trust myself to get the I2C bus right on the first try inside a program, and part of it was something I'd noticed on the team, whenever the one person who understood a specific part of the electrical system was missing, nobody else really knew what was going on with it. Not a communication issue exactly, just that everyone had their own piece and there wasn't much slack to go around. That messy notebook page ended up more useful than the actual schematic later. That's where I worked out I2C1 on GPIO26/27 with 4.7kΩ pull ups for 400kHz, figured out the MS5837 wiring (SDA comes out as orange, SCL as brown, no idea why sensor manufacturers do this), and mapped all five ESC channels to headers so I wasn't guessing which thruster was which while putting it together. Everything on the final board traces back to that one page, and now other people on the subsystem can actually use it too instead of just asking me.

# The Board That Doesn't Need a Legend

I watched the HTM Workshop's PCB design course on YouTube and decided to just set myself a deadline, because otherwise I'd keep putting it off. Learning KiCad from scratch and finishing an actual PCB by myself in a short window. I could've just asked a senior to help but wanted to do this one on my own. Gave myself five days as a buffer, figured two to learn the software and the rest to finish the design. Ended up doing it in three, two days learning KiCad and one day actually laying out the board and generating the Gerber file for Lion Circuits, our fabricator. First real PCB for the team, so it felt like a decent milestone, we might end up moving most of our other components off perfboard because of it.

The fabricated board is basically the same circuit, just not held together by tape and hope anymore. R3 and R4 sit right next to the RP2350-Zero as permanent I2C pull ups instead of floating loose like before. J1 through J4 are labelled directly on the silkscreen, so I don't have to remember which connector goes where anymore. What used to be a mess of colour coded wire is now just a trace you can actually follow.
