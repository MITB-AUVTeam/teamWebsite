Every AUV needs a brain, but a brain is useless without a nervous system. So, after discussing it with Azad and Chatur, we concluded that's what this board had to be: the central hub tying our microcontroller to the BNO055 IMU, five ESCs driving the thrusters, and the MS5837 pressure sensor. Three completely different signal diets, one small board, and zero tolerance for a loose connection meters underwater.

# Ugly Before Clean

We didn't start with a PCB. It felt out of reach at the stage we were brainstorming how to connect everything and minimize error, not to mention I had no prior experience working with, let alone designing, a PCB by myself. Fab houses don't do same-day returns, so failure wasn't an option on a strict budget. A perfboard lets you rip out a bad connection and redo it in the time it takes to reheat a joint, which meant instant course correction whenever something went wrong. So we embraced the chaos: wires everywhere, masking tape standing in for strain relief, pin identities living rent-free in my head instead of on the board. Before it could be clean, it had to be ugly.

# The GPIO That Cried Wolf

Midway through, our original RP2040-based Vicharak board decided to fail inexplicably. GPIO pins started ghosting us, no clear reason, no warning. Rather than play detective on a dying board, we cut our losses and moved to the Waveshare RP2350-Zero since we were already nearing our submission deadline. It turned out to be cheaper, sturdier, and refreshingly drama-free. It landed at the bottom edge of the perfboard, with the USB-C exposed for quick reflashing. We kept the original female headers for the Vicharak in place in case the RP2350 ran into similar issues, and it also saved us time. Five 2-pin JST connectors handled the ESCs, a 4-pin JST handled the IMU, and another 4-pin JST handled the pressure sensor. Small mercy: I kept colour discipline throughout, a habit picked up from the TinkerCAD circuit simulations the seniors had us run during task phase. Yellow, blue, green for signal; red and black for power and ground.

# Notebook Before KiCad

Before touching any PCB software, I sat down and hand-drew the whole circuit. Partly out of caution, since I didn't trust myself to get the I2C bus right on the first try inside a program, and partly because I'd noticed that whenever the one person handling a particular task on the electrical subsystem was absent, no one else had a clue how to pick it up: connections, usage, any of it. That wasn't a communication problem between us, just the nature of the work, where everyone was stretched to their capacity on their own piece. That rough schematic ended up more useful than the final design itself. It's where I worked out the I2C1 bus on GPIO26/27 with 4.7kΩ pull-ups sized for 400kHz, untangled the MS5837's wiring (its SDA arrives as orange, SCL as brown; sensor manufacturers apparently enjoy chaos too), and mapped all five ESC channels to their headers so I wouldn't be guessing which thruster was which mid-assembly. Everything on the fabricated board traces back to this one page, and it's easier now for other electrical subsystem members, or anyone from another subsystem, to reference while testing.

# The Board That Doesn't Need a Legend

After watching the HTM Workshop's PCB design course on YouTube, I set a hard deadline for the final product. That meant learning a completely new piece of software in a very short window and finishing the PCB by myself. I could have asked my seniors for help, but I wanted to take this one on. So I gave myself five days as grace, two to learn KiCad and the rest to complete the design, and ended up finishing in three: two days learning the software, and one day to design the board and generate the final Gerber file to Lion Circuits' (our PCB fabricator) specifications. Since this was our first proper PCB, it was a big step for the whole team. We may now be able to shift most, if not all, of our components onto proper PCBs and get rid of the hassle that comes with perfboard.

The fabricated version, made through Lion Circuits, is the same board, just no longer held together by hope and masking tape. R3 and R4 (resistors) sit right next to the RP2350-Zero as permanent I2C pull-ups instead of floating loose in the wiring. J1 through J4 are silkscreened directly onto the board, so pin identity now lives in the copper instead of in my memory. What used to be a rat's nest of colour-coded wire is now a routed trace you can actually follow with your eyes.
